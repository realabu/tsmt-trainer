## Purpose

This document captures motivation-related research interpretation and product implications for later TSMT Trainer discovery.

The research direction combines:

- motivation theory, especially autonomy, competence, and relatedness perspectives
- early-childhood self-regulation and age-appropriate meaning-making
- visual structure and predictability research traditions
- reward and reinforcement trade-offs
- gamification benefits and failure modes
- sensory discomfort, fatigue, anxiety, challenge, and regulation as possible alternatives to a simple "motivation problem" explanation
- the existing TSMT-specific product discovery and Evidence Map findings

The core purpose is not to prove that the current product idea works. It is to improve the quality of future questions and prevent the Motivation Toolbox from collapsing into a simple list of rewards or game mechanics.

## Research Interpretation Guardrails

Do not treat the following as established TSMT-specific facts:

- visible progress automatically reduces practice refusal
- explaining why an exercise matters automatically turns external pressure into intrinsic motivation
- visual timelines dramatically reduce refusal for all children
- token systems always work briefly and then devalue
- narrative, badges, or themes automatically sustain long-term motivation
- resistance should be treated as a motivation deficit
- sensory overload should be described with simplified "reptilian brain takes over" language

The more defensible current direction is:

- understandable structure and predictability may help some children orient themselves in a difficult process
- competence feedback may support engagement when it remains non-comparative and non-pressuring
- meaningful rationale may support internalization when it is age-appropriate and occurs in an autonomy-supportive, relationally safe context
- children differ substantially in what they experience as motivating, pressuring, distracting, frightening, or regulating
- refusal may signal fatigue, discomfort, fear, sensory load, task difficulty, uncertainty, relational conflict, or other barriers rather than a lack of motivation
- motivation mechanisms should be separated and tested rather than bundled into a fully gamified product

## Mechanism-Based Motivation Model

The Motivation Toolbox should be understood first as a set of motivation-support mechanisms.

### 1. Meaning

Core question:
What does this action mean to the child now?

Possible product direction:

- explain what is happening now
- connect the current activity to a close, understandable goal
- provide short, concrete, age-appropriate meaning
- avoid abstract therapeutic explanations that exceed the child's comprehension

Example tangible tools:

- short mission framing
- one-sentence purpose message
- story context
- simple before/after explanation
- parent-delivered meaning prompt

### 2. Autonomy

Core question:
What can the child safely own or choose without changing the professional TSMT plan?

Possible product direction:

- child-owned start/next/finish actions
- safe micro-choices
- choosing among available themes or presentation modes
- choosing a celebration or recognition form

Example tangible tools:

- big next button controlled by the child
- theme choice
- character choice
- mission choice where the underlying professional task remains unchanged
- choosing a physical reward or celebration from parent-approved options

Important boundary:
Do not create fake autonomy or allow the child-facing layer to change the professional meaning, order, repetition count, or execution requirements of the instructor-provided plan unless the professional plan itself allows such choice.

### 3. Competence

Core question:
Can the child feel that they can do this and that their own effort or ability is changing over time?

Possible product direction:

- understandable progress
- completed-session recognition
- nearest meaningful milestone
- self-comparison only where meaningful and safe
- constructive, informational feedback rather than pressure

Example tangible tools:

- visible task and session progress
- personal milestones
- badges as informational recognition
- streak-like structures only if they do not create fear of loss or shame
- personal-best representations where later discovery shows they are safe
- physical stickers representing genuine milestones

Important boundary:
Competence support must not become speed chasing, peer ranking, or a simplistic claim that faster execution means better therapeutic progress.

### 4. Predictability And Structure

Core question:
Does the child understand the sequence, current position, remaining work, and endpoint?

Possible product direction:

- visual sequence
- current task orientation
- visible remaining work
- clear finish point
- session and weekly structure at an age-appropriate information density

Example tangible tools:

- task strip
- progress path
- session map
- simple weekly goal view
- visual countdown of remaining tasks
- optional time orientation

Important boundary:
Visual structure may support predictability, but the product should not claim that a progress bar or visual schedule automatically solves refusal.

### 5. Relatedness And Shared Practice

Core question:
Does the product help the child experience practice as something done with a supportive parent rather than something imposed by an operator?

Possible product direction:

- shared parent-child progress world
- parent as Practice Partner
- shared start and finish rituals
- recognition and celebration together
- parent scaffolding rather than child-only app use

Example tangible tools:

- shared celebration
- parent-child mission framing
- joint check-off ritual
- physical high-five or celebration prompt
- shared story progression

Important boundary:
The product should support the relationship without prescribing a parenting doctrine or promising relationship improvement as an outcome.

### 6. Narrative And Identity

Core question:
Can a story, role, or theme make the practice feel personally meaningful without becoming the primary product value?

Possible product direction:

- themes as identity layers
- stories as optional meaning layers
- missions and characters as emotional framing

Example tangible tools:

- Space theme
- Dinosaurs theme
- Princesses theme
- Vehicles theme
- Animals theme
- astronaut training story
- explorer mission
- character-guided journey

Important boundary:
Narrative is optional support. It should not distract from physical practice or require high screen engagement.

### 7. Recognition And Achievement

Core question:
How can completed effort and meaningful milestones be recognized without buying compliance?

Possible product direction:

- informational recognition
- celebration of persistence and completion
- milestone recognition
- parent-curated acknowledgement

Example tangible tools:

- badges
- stickers
- certificates
- achievement cards
- physical progress boards
- small celebration rituals

Important boundary:
A badge can be either informational recognition or a controlling external reward. Later discovery should distinguish these uses.

### 8. External Rewards

Core question:
When, if ever, is an external reward useful as one optional support mechanism?

Possible product direction:

- parent-curated and optional
- not the foundation of the product
- not automatically escalated by the system
- not the only reason for participation

Example tangible tools:

- physical rewards
- parent-defined privileges
- stickers
- small jointly selected experiences

Important boundary:
The system should not become a reward inflation engine and should not prescribe food or screen-time rewards.

## Tangible Toolbox Examples Must Remain Visible

The mechanism-based model is intended to explain why and when a tool may help. It should not replace the existing concrete, understandable toolbox examples.

The future Motivation Toolbox should therefore preserve both levels:

### Motivation mechanisms

- Meaning
- Autonomy
- Competence
- Predictability and structure
- Relatedness and shared practice
- Narrative and identity
- Recognition and achievement
- External rewards

### Tangible examples

- Themes
- Badges
- Stories
- Characters
- Missions
- Visual progress paths
- Activity / task sequences
- Optional timing
- Personal milestones
- Stickers
- Certificates
- Shared celebration rituals
- Physical rewards
- Other optional motivational tools

The relationship between these levels is many-to-many.

For example:

- a Space theme may support identity, narrative, meaning, and relatedness
- a badge may support competence or recognition, but may also become a controlling external reward
- optional timing may support predictability or self-comparison, but may also create pressure or speed chasing
- a physical sticker may provide recognition, progress visibility, or external reward depending on how it is framed and used
- a story may support meaning and identity, but may also become distracting screen content if over-designed

The future concept model should therefore avoid classifying a concrete tool as inherently good or bad. Product intent, framing, child fit, timing, and observed effect matter.

## Motivation Problem Or Another Barrier?

Future Motivation / Reinforcement Discovery should explicitly avoid assuming that resistance is always a motivation problem.

Possible alternative or interacting barriers include:

- physical fatigue
- pain
- sensory discomfort
- fear or anxiety
- excessive challenge
- unclear execution
- uncertainty about what comes next
- cognitive overload
- tiredness after kindergarten or school
- conflict around parent-child roles
- sibling-related frustration
- preference for free play
- difficulty transitioning into structured activity

Product consequence:
The system should not automatically add more rewards when a child resists.

Future discovery should examine whether support belongs to:

- meaning
- predictability
- motivation
- execution guidance
- pause / stop handling
- parent observation
- instructor follow-up outside the immediate child-facing experience

The product must not diagnose sensory overload or autonomously modify the professional program.

## Screen-Light Motivation Principle

Motivation support should not turn TSMT Trainer into a conventional child entertainment app.

Current research-support direction:

- the screen should support physical action rather than compete with it
- interactions should be brief and glanceable
- avoid feeds, autoplay, infinite content, and attention-capture loops
- themes and narratives can exist without continuous animated screen engagement
- physical and shared parent-child rewards remain valid toolbox examples
- actual screen time and attention diversion should be observed during pilots

## Open Questions For Motivation / Reinforcement Discovery

- When is resistance primarily motivational, and when does it signal another barrier?
- Which mechanisms support participation across different ages and child profiles?
- Which safe choices can the child own without changing the professional program?
- What kinds of competence feedback are motivating without creating pressure?
- When do badges function as informational recognition, and when as controlling reward?
- Which narrative layers remain useful after novelty fades?
- When is optional timing helpful, neutral, or harmful?
- How should the parent decide which tool to introduce and when?
- How can the product avoid automatic diagnosis and one-size-fits-all assumptions?
- Which tools should be entirely screen-light or physical?

## Open Questions For Meaning / Education Discovery

- What can children around ages 3, 5, 7, and 9 meaningfully understand about the purpose of practice?
- Is "what are we doing now?" more useful than "why are we doing this?" at some ages?
- How close and concrete should purpose explanations be?
- Which meaning belongs in child-facing communication and which belongs only to the parent?
- Can meaning support internalization without becoming persuasive pressure?

## Open Questions For Execution / Practice Support Discovery

- Which resistance signals should cause the parent to pause rather than motivate harder?
- What neutral pause / stop behavior is useful?
- How can the product support safe observation without diagnosing the child?
- What lightweight observations would be useful later for parent reflection or instructor context?

## Open Questions For Motivation Configuration Discovery

- Which motivation preferences should the parent configure?
- Which stimuli should be separately controllable: sound, animation, timing, competition, narrative intensity?
- How much configuration is helpful before setup itself becomes a burden?
- Should configuration be preference-based rather than diagnosis-based?
- How should motivation layers be introduced gradually over time?

## Pilot Implications

Do not test a fully gamified bundle first.

Prefer separable mechanism tests such as:

1. Minimal Runner
   - current task
   - sequence
   - finishability
   - next action

2. Visible Progress
   - remaining tasks
   - session progress
   - simple weekly progress where appropriate

3. Safe Choice Layer
   - limited child-owned choices that do not alter the professional plan

4. Narrative Layer
   - theme, mission, or story support

5. Achievement Layer
   - milestone badge, sticker, certificate, or shared celebration

6. Optional Timing
   - orientation or self-comparison only where safe and meaningful

Potential observed outcomes:

- practice-start resistance
- interruptions
- refusal language
- repeated asking about when practice ends
- parent cognitive load
- parent-child conflict perception
- child pride or competence expression
- screen attention diversion
- speed-chasing or pressure behavior
- novelty fade over time

- Badges
- Stories
- Characters
- Physical rewards
- Other optional motivational tools

The research input adds a mechanism layer around these examples rather than replacing them.

## Evidence Confidence Summary

### Strong theoretical fit, still requiring TSMT-specific validation

- autonomy support
- competence support
- related parent-child practice
- child differences and need for flexible motivation support

### Moderate / indirect support

- predictable visual structure
- age-appropriate meaning and rationale
- progress information as competence support
- parent scaffolding around self-regulation

### Open or context-dependent

- long-term narrative effect
- badge effect
- timing effect
- specific sequencing of motivation layers
- effect of visible weekly/cycle progress on resistance

### Explicit risk areas

- reward inflation
- controlling reward framing
- peer competition
- speed chasing
- screen distraction
- fake autonomy
- treating all refusal as lack of motivation
- using motivational pressure to override genuine discomfort
